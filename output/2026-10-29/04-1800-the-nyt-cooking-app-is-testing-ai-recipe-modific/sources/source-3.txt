Laptops with OLED screens aren’t particularly special. They’ve been around for a decade. You can currently buy a touchscreen OLED machine for as little as $700, and even the iPad Pro has had an OLED screen since 2024.
So, why did Apple wait over a decade to release its own touchscreen OLED MacBook Pro? The simple answer for the long wait is that the company wanted to do it “the Apple way.” But in this case, “the Apple way” involves somehow cranking up the display to 2,000 nits, solving both the brightness and glare problems that OLED laptops have always suffered from.
Having seen the thing with my own eyes at a media event today in Los Angeles, I can tell you that this screen is the real deal.
Like No Other OLED
Apple is using a tandem OLED panel, the same layer-stacking technology used in the OLED iPad Pro. The real innovation here, however, is that the panel has been optimized to hit a new peak brightness of 2,000 nits, and that’s a result that matters.
That big number is meaningless on its own, but here’s the context. Nits are the standard measure of screen brightness used across the consumer tech industry. A cheap OLED laptop maxes out around 300 nits. A higher-end OLED laptop might get you up to 1,000 nits of brightness—but only in brighter and more colorful High Dynamic Range content like specific streaming apps, YouTube videos, and games. In other words, the vast majority of the time you’re using an OLED, it’ll feel like a dimmer screen than normal. The iPad Pro can get up to 1,600 nits for HDR content, and 1,000 nits for the less bright, less colorful Standard Dynamic Range content. That’s closer.
But this MacBook Pro’s screen is on an entirely different level. It’s a ground-up redesign of the tandem OLED layer arrangement in the iPad Pro, even down to the manufacturing process. And the result is that it can blast your eyes with 2,000 nits of brightness in both SDR and HDR. That means whether you’re looking at some generic wallpaper, a website, or an HDR-compatible movie, you’ll feel that extra brightness. That’s why, I assume, Apple gave it the name PROLED XDR. In a world where the words “OLED” and “HDR” don’t necessarily indicate how good the experience is, more weighty jargon is needed.
As with all tech, bigger numbers don’t always add up to a noticeable difference for the user. But when it comes to HDR performance, we’ve been waiting a long time for OLED to catch up.
HDR, or High Dynamic Range, serves as a measure of color depth, brightness, and contrast—and in the past, this has always involved a trade-off. An OLED screen can turn each individual pixel any color, and it can turn off individual pixels in the display. This allows OLED to have great color control and unbeatable contrast, since when you turn a pixel off, it renders absolutely black.
But OLEDs couldn’t get super bright, and other screen tech like mini-LED could get much brighter, which is why Apple stuck with mini-LED for years, using it on the MacBook Pro and Studio Display XDR. This technology allowed Apple to have the laptops with the best HDR performance out there.
With the 2026 MacBook Pro, that compromise no longer exists. This OLED screen has both the brightness and contrast in its pocket, and you can see it.
An OLED panel has lots of more benefits over conventional backlighting beyond just contrast. Mini-LED struggles with an effect called blooming, where bright parts of images bleed light to the surrounding area, which is where lots of detail is lost in the image. OLED completely fixes that, giving each pixel a much wider range of control (up to completely turning off). As part of the Apple event I attended, we were shown a selection of HDR images with lots of contrast, a number of which had fields of darkness at the edges that made it look like the image simply disappeared into the bezel. Very pretty. We also watched a darker scene in the show Widow’s Bay in HDR, which was an excellent showcase of the range of detail this screen can show.
The new screen helps this MacBook stay exceedingly light, as well. Much of the savings in weight comes from the OLED design, since the screen uses thinner and lighter materials than screens past. Despite the computer using the same aluminum chassis as the previous MacBook Pro, the new 14-inch MacBook Pro weighs just 3 pounds, which is 12 percent lighter than before.
To put the new screen into context, we were also shown a comparison between Apple’s OLED and the OLED of a high-end competitive Windows laptop. The difference was striking, especially in off-axis viewing. At its worst, there were visible rainbow effects on the Windows machine’s screen.
There’s a catch, though. While Apple’s screen can get up to 2,000 nits, it’s normally capped at 1,000 nits. When the laptop’s built-in adaptive brightness sensor thinks you need a boost, the screen’s brightens increases up to 2,000 nits. So the brightness only hits the high end when you’re outside or working under really harsh lighting conditions. The previous-gen MacBook Pros also did this, but to a lesser degree. With this machine, you’re getting 400 more nits of SDR brightness right out of the box, with the ability to crank it up to twice as much.
Glare is another issue with conventional, low-brightness OLED laptops that the MacBook Pro improves. When deeper blacks are rendered on the screen, it often results in harsher reflections. Sometimes it can get so bad, it feels like you’re looking into a mirror. The brightness alone on the MacBook Pro goes a long way toward overpowering glare and reflections, but Apple is also using what it claims is a “state-of-the-art” anti-reflective coating. Almost every laptop screen uses some kind anti-reflective coating, so one can hope that Apple’s “state-of-the-art” coating is at least on par with those, but I can attest that this is the very best I’ve seen.
The ultimate solution to cutting glare and reflections is still the option for a nano-texture display, where the anti-reflective pattern is actually etched into the screen itself rather than layered on top. That’s available as an upgrade on both the 14-inch and 16-inch models.
Reach Out and Touch It
The other major innovation happening with the MacBook Pro’s screen is the introduction of touch. Like OLED itself, this is a feature we’ve seen on many other laptops for well over a decade. I came in hoping Apple would swing for the fences here, but its use of touch interaction feels much more modest. (Let us quickly forget the failure of the Touch Bar from 2016.)
The implementation itself is impressive. Interacting with elements on the screen feels every bit as fluid as it does on an iPad. The screen also supports many of the familiar trackpad gestures, such as pinch to zoom or a three-fingered swipe to switch Spaces.
The most natural feeling touch optimizations have been lifted directly from iPadOS. If you tap in the top left window controls, they zoom in to let you select more precisely. You can even grab a window and flick it to the side to quickly get into Split View. My favorite optimization is the ability to tap, hold, and slide down to select an item within the Menu Bar. Very clever. And yet, I was still hoping for a stronger commitment on the software side. You can’t use an Apple Pencil on this screen, and nothing in the hardware of the MacBook Pro has been adjusted to make touching the screen feel any more ergonomic.
The bigger issue is with third-party apps, though. While touch-to-click will work on all apps automatically, it’s up to developers to do any more than that. Even the third-party apps we were shown during demos had varying degrees of touch optimization.
The 3D modeling application ZBrush was a good example. While certain aspects were touch-enabled, none of the controls or menus had the useful optimizations that Apple’s first-party apps had. Hopefully this touch option will become more widely adopted, but I can foresee a future in which these fingers-first interactions are mostly forgotten outside of occasionally scrolling down a webpage with your thumb. And let me be clear: All of this is more than what Microsoft has ever done to optimize Windows 11 for touch. (And Microsoft even has Windows 2-in-1 tablets to support!)
With the 2026 MacBook Pro, Apple is doubling down on what’s working well for it: leading the way in high-end hardware. There’s plenty more testing to do on the MacBook Pro in the coming weeks, but this OLED screen already looks like the one the entire industry will be chasing for years to come.
